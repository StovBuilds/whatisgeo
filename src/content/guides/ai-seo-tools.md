Before you compare a single product, decide which of the two groups you're shopping in and which job you need done. Most disappointment with these tools starts with buying the wrong kind for the problem you actually have.

## What people mean by AI SEO tools

The phrase covers two different groups of product sold under one label.

The first group is familiar SEO software: keyword research, page analysis, site crawling, content briefs. All of that existed before, and much of it now has an AI assistant added. The second group is newer. These tools put questions to AI systems such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Perplexity and record whether your business turns up in the answers, how it is described and which sources are cited.

This page doesn't rank products. We have no method we could show you that would back a "best" list, so we sort tools by job instead. If you want the wider definition first, [what is AI SEO](/what-is-ai-seo/) covers it.

## The main kinds of AI SEO tool, by job

Four jobs cover almost everything on the market.

| Job | What the tool does | Where AI usually appears |
|---|---|---|
| Research | Finds the queries people use, topics you're missing and what competitors cover | Grouping queries into themes, suggesting topics, summarising data |
| Content | Outlines, drafts, edits and scores pages | Drafting and rewriting copy, writing briefs |
| Technical | Crawls your site for broken links, slow pages and indexing problems | Explaining issues in plain sentences, suggesting fixes |
| AI-answer tracking | Asks AI systems a fixed set of questions and logs mentions and citations | The AI system is the thing being measured |

Plenty of products cover two or three of these jobs. That's fine, as long as you know which job you're hiring for before you read a feature list. A tool that's good at drafting does nothing for a crawl problem, and a crawler won't tell you what AI answers say about you.

## What AI is and is not doing inside these tools

In most products the AI is doing language work: summarising a pile of data, suggesting topics, drafting copy, grouping queries and turning a report into readable sentences. That saves time on the dull parts.

What it doesn't have is knowledge of your business. It doesn't know which services make you money, which customers you want or which claims on your page are accurate. Google's own guidance on generative AI content makes the underlying point: generative models "don't retrieve facts, but predict a likely sequence of words". So a suggested keyword may have little real demand behind it, and a drafted paragraph may say something untrue about your product.

The working rule (our recommendation): treat AI output as a fast first pass, check it, and keep a person who knows the business in the review step. A confident-sounding tool can still be wrong.

## Free tools worth starting with

You can start without paying anyone, and the first tools to set up are documented by the search engines themselves.

**Google Search Console.** Google describes it as a free service. Its Performance report shows clicks, impressions, click-through rate and average position, and you can break those down by the queries that brought people to your site. That is real data on how you already appear, which no third-party estimate can match.

**Bing Webmaster Tools.** Microsoft's equivalent for Bing. In February 2026 Microsoft announced an AI Performance report in public preview, which shows how often your pages are cited as sources in Microsoft Copilot, AI-generated summaries in Bing and some partner integrations, plus the queries the AI used to retrieve your content. Microsoft notes that these figures show citation activity, not ranking or importance, and that the query data is a sample. We couldn't find a Microsoft page that states the sign-up terms in plain text, so check them when you register.

Neither is sold as an "AI" product, and that is useful: they show what is already happening on your own site before you spend anything. Set both up first and you'll know which problem you're actually trying to solve.

Paid products often have free tiers, but we haven't checked any vendor's current terms for this page, so we don't name them. If you try one, read the vendor's own pricing page and note the date you checked.

## AI content tools: what Google says and where the risk sits

This is the worry that stops many small businesses, so here is what Google actually documents.

Google's 2023 guidance says "appropriate use of AI or automation is not against our guidelines". What it does treat as spam is using automation, including AI, "with the primary purpose of manipulating ranking in search results". Its spam policies define scaled content abuse as generating many pages mainly to manipulate rankings and not to help users, and they list "using generative AI tools or other similar tools to generate many pages without adding value for users" as an example.

So Google cares about the purpose and value of the pages, whatever wrote them. Publishing a hundred thin, near-identical pages is the risk. Using a drafting tool to speed up a page that a knowledgeable person then checks and improves is a different activity. Google's generative AI guidance also says to fact-check and review all AI-generated content before publishing, including titles, meta descriptions, structured data and image alt text.

Following this guidance keeps you within Google's policies. It doesn't promise a ranking. For the fuller quality question, see [is AI content good for SEO](/is-ai-content-good-for-seo/).

## Tools that track visibility in AI answers

This is the newer half of the phrase. Trackers put a set of questions to AI systems and log whether your business is mentioned, how it is described and which sources are cited.

The useful part is the record. Without one you're relying on someone's screenshot; with one you can see change over time. For how to set up measurement, including a manual method, see [how to measure AI visibility](/how-to-measure-ai-visibility/), and for a closer look at this category and the questions to ask vendors, see [generative engine optimisation tools](/generative-engine-optimization-tools/).

The limit, stated plainly: nobody can guarantee a ranking or a citation in AI answers, and no tool changes that. A tracker reports on what the AI system did; it doesn't control what it does next.

Google's documentation on AI features says there are no additional requirements or special optimisations needed to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, and that you don't need new machine-readable files, AI text files or special structured data. Be wary of any product that sells a special switch for Google's AI features.

There is research on how content changes affect visibility in AI answers. Aggarwal et al. ("GEO: Generative Engine Optimization", accepted to KDD 2024) built a benchmark of queries across several domains, tested content changes against generative engines in their own setup, and reported visibility gains of up to 40%, with the most effective changes varying by domain. That is a finding from a defined experimental setup and is no forecast for your site. If you want to check your own position step by step, the [AI visibility audit](/ai-visibility-audit/) guide walks through it.

## How to choose: a checklist you can run

This is our recommendation, not platform guidance.

1. **Name the job.** Research, content, technical or tracking. Write it down.
2. **Start with the free data.** Set up Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools first.
3. **Trial on your own site.** A demo on someone else's data proves little.
4. **Verify the output.** Check a sample of suggestions against what you know to be true.
5. **Keep human review.** Someone who knows the business signs off before anything goes live.
6. **Prefer exportable data.** If you can't take your data with you, you lose your history when you leave.
7. **Check the vendor's own pages.** For prices and terms, use the vendor's site and note the date you looked.
8. **Ask what they can't promise.** A vendor guaranteeing rankings or citations is promising something nobody controls.

## Common mistakes when buying AI SEO tools

**Buying before naming the job.** You end up with a drafting tool when the real issue was a crawl error.

**Trusting a "best of" list with no stated method.** If you can't see how the ranking was made, you can't weigh it.

**Publishing unchecked output.** Fast and wrong is still wrong, and Google tells you to review AI content before it goes live.

**Expecting a guarantee.** Some marketing implies one. No tool can deliver it.

**Skipping the free tools.** Search Console already shows your queries, clicks and impressions. Paying for a dashboard before looking at that is backwards.

**Skipping a baseline.** Without one, you can't tell whether anything worked. The [measurement section of our guide](/#measurement) is a good place to start.
