The deciding question is why a page exists and what a reader gets from it. Google documents this clearly, and it also tells you to fact-check AI output before publishing, a step most flat "yes" or "no" answers leave out.

## Three kinds of claim, kept apart

Most answers to this question are opinions. A vendor says yes because it sells the tools, and a forum thread says no because someone's traffic fell. Neither is evidence. This page sticks to three kinds of claim and labels each one:

1. **What Google documents.** The firmest ground, because Google sets the rules for its own Search.
2. **Research.** This page cites no study and uses no traffic or ranking statistics, because we only use research we have read and can put in context.
3. **Sensible practice.** Where something is our recommendation, we say so.

Keeping these apart clears up most of the confusion. "Google penalises AI" mixes a real spam policy with rumour. "AI is fine, publish at will" ignores that same policy. Both make the same mistake: they treat the production method as the whole story.

## What Google documents about AI-generated content

Google sets out its position in a Search Central blog post from February 2023, "Google Search's guidance about AI-generated content", and in a documentation page on using generative AI content. The key lines:

- **Quality is the focus, however content is made.** The blog post says Google focuses on "the quality of content, rather than how content is produced".
- **Appropriate use is allowed.** From its FAQ: "Appropriate use of AI or automation is not against our guidelines."
- **Manipulation is spam.** Using automation, including AI, to generate content with the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings is, in Google's words, "a violation of our spam policies".
- **AI earns nothing special.** "Using AI doesn't give content any special gains. It's just content." If it is useful, helpful, original and shows aspects of E-E-A-T, "it might do well in Search. If it doesn't, it might not."
- **Review is expected.** The documentation page says generative models "don't retrieve facts, but predict a likely sequence of words", so their outputs may contain inaccuracies, and that "it is critical to manually factcheck and review all AI-generated content for accuracy and trustworthiness before publishing". That review covers metadata too: title elements, meta descriptions, structured data and image alt text.

That last point matters. Fact-checking AI output is something Google itself tells you to do, in writing.

## Where AI content gets into trouble: scaled content abuse

Google's spam policies define scaled content abuse as "when many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users", typically "large amounts of unoriginal content that provides little to no value to users, no matter how it's created".

The listed examples include:

- using generative AI tools or similar tools to generate many pages without adding value for users
- scraping feeds, search results or other content to generate many pages, including through automated synonymising, translating or other obfuscation
- stitching together content from different pages without adding value
- creating multiple sites to hide the scaled nature of the content
- creating many pages that make little or no sense to a reader but contain search keywords

Notice the phrase "no matter how it's created". The policy also covers human-written pages made in bulk for the same purpose. AI comes up so often because it makes producing many pages cheap and fast, which is the pattern the policy describes. The same spam policies page also counts attempts "to manipulate generative AI responses in Google Search" as spam.

There is a quieter risk below the spam line. Google's people-first guidance lists warning signs of "search engine-first content", including "Are you using extensive automation to produce content on many topics?" and "Are you mainly summarizing what others have to say without adding much value?" A page can stay clear of every spam policy and still be too generic to earn anything.

## What helpful content looks like, and where AI drafts fall short

Google's guidance on helpful, reliable, people-first content gives self-assessment questions that read like an editor's checklist. Among them: does the content provide original information, reporting, research or analysis? Does it have any easily verified factual errors? Is it written or reviewed by an expert or enthusiast who demonstrably knows the topic? Does it show first-hand expertise, "for example, expertise that comes from having actually used a product or service, or visiting a place"?

The same page explains E-E-A-T (experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness). It says trust is the most important of the four, and that E-E-A-T "itself isn't a specific ranking factor". It also suggests asking "Who, How, and Why" about your content, and describes the "Why" as "perhaps the most important question". Content made primarily to help people aligns with what Google's systems seek to reward; content made primarily to attract search visits does not.

Our view, offered as practice and not as Google's rule: those questions are exactly where unedited AI drafts struggle.

- A model has not used the product, visited the place or run the test. It can describe those things fluently, and fluent is not the same as accurate.
- Drafts tend to summarise what already exists, so they rarely add anything new.
- Nobody is accountable for a draft until a named person puts their name to it.

None of that makes AI unusable. It means the human review stage is essential.

## Do you need to disclose AI use?

The Search Central pages we read do not require a disclosure on ordinary web pages. They recommend one where it makes sense. Google's FAQ says AI or automation disclosures "are useful for content where someone might think 'How was this created?'" and suggests adding them "when it would be reasonably expected". For content substantially generated by automation, the people-first page adds questions such as whether that use is "self-evident to visitors through disclosures or in other ways". The FAQ also says giving AI an author byline "is probably not the best way" to make AI's role clear to readers.

Shopping has a firmer rule, and it is written down. Google's generative AI page says that for ecommerce sites, Merchant Center requires AI-generated images to carry IPTC `TrainedAlgorithmicMedia` metadata, and requires AI-generated product titles and descriptions to be labelled as AI-generated. If you sell through Merchant Center, read its own policy.

Our recommendation: where readers would reasonably wonder how a page was made, a short note on how AI was used and who reviewed it costs little.

## A review checklist before you publish

The first item is Google's documented advice. The rest are our recommendation; treat them as the minimum.

1. **Fact-check every claim**, plus the title, meta description, structured data and alt text. If you cannot source a claim, cut it.
2. **Add first-hand detail.** What did you or your team actually see, run, measure or get wrong?
3. **Name the expertise.** Say who wrote or reviewed the page and why they know the topic.
4. **Edit for originality.** Ask what this page says that the pages already ranking do not. If the answer is nothing, rethink it.
5. **Check the "why".** Would you publish this page for your existing readers if search engines did not exist?
6. **Do not bulk-publish without review.** No batch of pages should go live until a person has read each one.

## Does this change for AI Overviews, ChatGPT and Perplexity?

For Google, no. Google's page on AI features says the same foundational SEO best practices apply to AI Overviews and AI Mode as to Search overall, including creating helpful, reliable, people-first content. To be shown as a supporting link, a page must be indexed and eligible to appear in Search with a snippet, and "there are no additional technical requirements". It also says "specific optimization isn't required". Our guide to [Google AI Overviews](/how-to-rank-in-google-ai-overviews/) goes into more detail.

For ChatGPT, Perplexity and other assistants, this page quotes no documentation on AI-written content, and we will not invent any. As practice and no more: accurate, clearly sourced pages with a named author seem sensible to aim for if you want to be cited, and nothing guarantees a mention. ## What nobody can tell you

Nobody can promise that an AI-assisted page will rank, or that it will not. Google publishes its policies and its intent, never a formula, so treat any claim of certainty as a sales line, whoever makes it.

What you can do is publish carefully, then watch your own results and adjust. The [measurement section](/#measurement) of our main guide explains how to track progress in search and in AI answers.
