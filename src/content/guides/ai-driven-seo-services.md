Before you compare providers, find out which of the two meanings a proposal uses, because the deliverables, the risks and the way you measure them all differ. This guide sets out what Google documents, what research shows and what is just sales copy, then gives you questions to ask and red flags to watch for.

## Two things the phrase can mean

The first meaning is about **method**. The provider uses AI tools to do the work: keyword clustering, content briefs and drafts, audits, internal linking suggestions. What you get is still ordinary SEO, done with different tools.

The second meaning is about **target**. Here the service tries to get your business named, described or cited in generated answers, such as a ChatGPT reply or a Google AI Overview. That is a different goal and needs different measurement. For more on the second meaning, see [what is AI SEO](/what-is-ai-seo/) and our guide to hiring a [generative engine optimisation agency](/generative-engine-optimization-agency/).

Providers often blur the two. A proposal can say "AI-driven" and mean either, both, or neither once you read the scope. So the first job is to make them say which.

## What AI-driven SEO services typically include

There is no standard scope. What follows are common patterns from our reading of this market, so treat it as a description and not a definition. Expect some mix of:

1. Technical and content audits, often run with tools.
2. Keyword and topic research, often clustered with AI.
3. Content briefs or full drafts written with language models.
4. On-page changes: titles, headings, internal links, structured data.
5. Reporting. For the second meaning, this may include checks on how AI answers mention your brand.

Some providers chain these tasks into automated workflows. That can save time, but it makes review more important, because errors get produced faster and in bulk.

## What Google documents about AI-generated content and scaled pages

This is the one part of the subject where there are written rules, so it is worth reading them in Google's own words.

**AI use by itself is allowed.** Google's 2023 guidance on AI-generated content says "appropriate use of AI or automation is not against our guidelines", and that its focus is on "the quality of content, rather than how content is produced". It also says using AI "doesn't give content any special gains".

**Scaled content abuse is not allowed, however it is made.** Google's spam policies define scaled content abuse as many pages "generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users", producing little or no value "no matter how it's created". The first example on the list is "using generative AI tools or other similar tools to generate many pages without adding value for users". Google says sites that violate its spam policies "may rank lower in results or not appear in results at all". The same page also counts attempts to manipulate generative AI responses in Google Search as spam.

**Review is expected.** Google's guidance on generative AI content says it is "critical to manually factcheck and review all AI-generated content for accuracy and trustworthiness before publishing", and that this applies to titles, meta descriptions, structured data and image alt text as well.

**AI Overviews and AI Mode have no extra requirements.** Google's page on AI features says a page must be indexed and eligible to show in Search with a snippet, and "there are no additional technical requirements". It adds that "specific optimization isn't required" and that normal SEO fundamentals still apply.

How this maps onto a proposal:

| What the service proposes | What Google's documentation implies |
|---|---|
| AI-assisted drafts, edited and fact-checked by a person | Allowed; Google judges the quality of the result |
| Hundreds of templated pages that differ mainly by keyword | Matches the scaled content abuse pattern if the pages add little for users |
| AI-written titles, descriptions and schema pushed live unreviewed | Google says to review these before publishing |
| A special technique for getting into AI Overviews or AI Mode | Google documents no extra technical requirements |

We cover the content question in more depth in [is AI content good for SEO](/is-ai-content-good-for-seo/).

## What is research and what is sales claim

Very little in this market is established fact, so be strict.

There is real research. Aggarwal et al., "GEO: Generative Engine Optimization", accepted to KDD 2024, built a benchmark called GEO-bench, made up of user queries across many domains along with web sources to answer them. In that benchmark, the authors report that their methods could raise visibility in generative engine responses by up to 40%. They also report that how well the methods worked varied by domain. That is a result under controlled test conditions. It does not tell you what will happen to your site, in your sector, on a live product that changes over time.

Treat everything else as a claim until you see the evidence. That includes case studies with no method, dashboards with no definitions, and "proprietary" models offered in place of evidence. Some of these claims may be true, but if you cannot check a claim, it is no reason to sign.

## Questions to ask before you sign

These are recommendations, not documented requirements.

1. Which meaning of "AI-driven" do you mean: AI-assisted delivery, visibility in AI answers, or both?
2. Which parts of the work do people do, and who reviews and fact-checks anything published under my name?
3. How do you make sure every page you publish adds something for a reader that the others do not?
4. What will you measure, how, and how often? Can I see the definitions and keep my own copy of the data?
5. What access do you need, and what can you change without asking me?
6. Which tools and models do you use, and does any of my content or data go into them?
7. What happens to the content and the data if we stop working together?

A good provider can answer all of these plainly.

## Red flags in proposals

Again, these are recommendations, though some tie back to Google's documentation.

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| A guarantee of rankings, traffic or AI citations | Nobody outside the platforms controls the results |
| Large-scale page production as the main deliverable | Can match Google's scaled content abuse pattern |
| No named person reviewing content before it goes live | Google says AI output should be fact-checked by hand before publishing |
| A special technique for Google's AI features | Google documents no extra technical requirements beyond normal Search eligibility |
| Vague scope, such as "AI optimisation", with no list of deliverables | You cannot check what you paid for |
| Results shown without method, baseline or dates | You cannot tell the provider's effect from normal variation |
| Refusal to name the tools or models used | You cannot judge data handling or quality control |

One of these may just mean a badly written proposal. Several together suggest a provider to avoid.

## How to judge whether it is working

Agree on the measures before work starts. For standard SEO, that means search visibility, clicks, and the pages that bring the outcomes you care about. Google says traffic from AI Overviews and AI Mode is included in the Search Console Performance report under the Web search type, so no separate AI figure exists for a provider to point to. Our home guide covers [measurement](/#measurement) in more detail.

For other AI answers, it is harder. Answers vary between runs and between platforms, so one screenshot proves very little. As a sensible practice, track a fixed set of buyer questions over time. Note whether your business is named and whether it is described accurately. Compare against a baseline you recorded before the work began. Our guide on [how to measure AI visibility](/how-to-measure-ai-visibility/) walks through the method.

Ask for a change log too: what changed, when and why. Without one, you cannot link any movement to the work.

If the proposal covers crawler access, check each platform's own documentation and avoid relying on the provider's summary. Our guide to [AI crawlers and robots.txt](/ai-crawlers-robots-txt/) links to those documents.

## When you may not need a service at all

Sometimes the honest answer is that you do not need one. Google says the same SEO fundamentals apply to its AI features: crawling allowed in robots.txt, content findable through internal links, important content in text form, and structured data that matches what is on the page. If your site is small, your offer is clear and someone on the team can write well, doing these basics yourself may go further than a retainer.

A service is worth paying for when you lack the time, skills or capacity and can name the specific gap. If you cannot name the gap, you may be paying for reassurance more than for work.
