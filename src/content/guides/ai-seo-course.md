Before you compare courses, decide which of the two skills you are paying to learn. Then judge the syllabus on whether its claims can be checked. A course that cannot show where its advice comes from is asking you to trust it, and this subject changes too often for that to be safe.

## What people mean by an AI SEO course

The phrase is used for two different things, and many course pages mix them.

The first is using AI tools to do SEO tasks faster: grouping keywords, drafting briefs, summarising audit data, writing first drafts. The skill here is a workflow with review built in, because the output still needs someone who knows what good looks like. Whether machine-written pages help or hurt is covered in [is AI content good for SEO](/is-ai-content-good-for-seo/).

The second is optimising for AI answers: getting your pages cited, linked or described accurately in answers from Google AI Overviews and AI Mode, ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude and others. That is what this site calls generative engine optimisation, and it is a separate job from the first.

Plenty of people need both. Check which one a course actually teaches before you sign up, because a title that says "AI SEO" can mean either.

## What a good course should cover

These are the topics we would expect. Most can be checked against a document the platform publishes itself.

**The foundations are ordinary SEO.** Google's guidance on AI features says you can apply the same foundational SEO best practices to AI Overviews and AI Mode as to Search overall. To be eligible to appear as a supporting link, a page must be indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet, and Google says there are no additional technical requirements. A course that skips this, or hides it under a new acronym, is starting in the wrong place.

**AI-assisted content, with its limits.** Google's 2023 guidance says appropriate use of AI or automation is not against its guidelines, while using it to generate content mainly to manipulate rankings breaks its spam policies. Its current page on generative AI content warns that generating many pages without adding value for users may breach the scaled content abuse policy, and says it is critical to fact-check and review AI-generated content before publishing. A good course teaches that review step. One that shows you how to publish hundreds of pages with no checking is teaching you to break a published policy.

**Crawler access, matched to each company's own documentation.** OpenAI, Anthropic and Perplexity each publish pages describing their crawlers, and they separate crawling for search from crawling for model training. OpenAI, for example, lists OAI-SearchBot for search in ChatGPT and GPTBot for training, and says each setting is independent. Anthropic lists Claude-SearchBot, Claude-User and ClaudeBot. Perplexity says PerplexityBot surfaces sites in its search results and is not used to crawl content for AI foundation models. A crawler module that treats "the AI bot" as one thing has not read these pages. Our [guide to AI crawlers and robots.txt](/ai-crawlers-robots-txt/) links all three.

**Measurement.** A course should show you how to tell whether anything changed, from referral traffic in your analytics to checking AI answers for the questions your buyers ask. See [how to measure AI visibility](/how-to-measure-ai-visibility/) for what can and cannot be measured.

## How to judge a course syllabus

We are not naming a "best" course, because we have no tested method that would back a ranking. What follows is our recommendation for judging any course yourself.

1. **Ask for the syllabus before you pay.** If the provider will not share a module list, that tells you something.
2. **Check when it was last updated.** Platform documentation changes, so an undated course is a weak sign.
3. **Check that each claim names a source.** Platform rules should point to the platform's own page. "Google says" with no link is not enough.
4. **Check how research is used.** The paper that introduced the term is Aggarwal et al., "GEO: Generative Engine Optimization" (KDD 2024). The authors tested ways of rewriting content on GEO-bench, a benchmark of 10,000 queries, using a generative engine they built from the top five Google results and a language model, and also ran tests on Perplexity. They report visibility gains of up to 40% in their setup, and say the effect varied across domains. A good course gives you those conditions. One that quotes "up to 40%" as a promise for your site has misread the paper.
5. **Prefer courses that teach measurement,** including how to set a baseline before you change anything.
6. **Treat any guarantee of inclusion as a red flag** (more on this below).

You cannot verify every line of a syllabus. You can check whether its claims are checkable, which is most of the work.

## Red flags that a course is selling hype

**A guarantee.** No course can promise your site will appear in ChatGPT answers or Google AI Overviews. Google's own wording is about eligibility: meeting the requirements makes a page eligible to be shown, which is a long way from being shown. Nobody outside the platform controls the outcome.

**A hidden method.** If the pitch is a technique the platforms supposedly do not want you to know, ask why it appears in no documentation. The platforms publish a lot about how they crawl and what they require, and a course should be able to point to it.

**Confident numbers with no trail.** Statistics with no named study, no conditions and no date. Real research comes with its limits attached, as the GEO paper does.

**No measurement module.** If a course cannot tell you how you would know it worked, you have no way to judge what it taught.

**An "official" certificate.** We found no GEO or AI SEO certification issued by Google, OpenAI, Anthropic or Perplexity in the platform documentation we read. Course providers and vendor academies do issue their own certificates; check who issues one before treating it as a credential.

## Free courses and vendor academies: what they are good for

Several software companies run free training on this topic. It is vendor material, made with the vendor's own product in view, and it can still be useful. We describe each only as its own page did when we checked it on 23 October 2026.

- **HubSpot Academy** has a learning path called "SEO in the Age of AI". Its page describes six playlists on answer engine optimisation, covering how search behaviour has changed, Google, ChatGPT, Perplexity and Gemini, AI content tools, video, and implementation and measurement. The page says the path ties this to HubSpot's own Loop Marketing framework, and the Academy describes its courses as free.
- **Semrush Academy** lists its courses under "Free Online Marketing Courses", including "AI Search Operating System" and "On-Page SEO and AI Search Essentials with Semrush". The second title names Semrush itself, a reminder that vendor courses are built around the vendor's tools.
- **Coursera** is a marketplace with courses from many providers. A search for "ai seo" there returns courses and specialisations that were marked "Free trial" when we checked.

Use free courses for vocabulary, a first look at the tools and a sense of what each company thinks matters. Do not expect a neutral answer on whether you need that company's software. Test what they teach against the platform documentation, as you would with a paid course.

## Course, tool, agency or doing it yourself

A course is one option among several, and the right one depends on what you are short of.

| If you are short of | Consider | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge | A course, or the platform documentation itself | Claims with no source |
| Time on repetitive tasks | A tool; see [AI SEO tools](/ai-seo-tools/) | Output nobody reviews |
| Capacity or in-house skill | An agency; see [choosing a GEO agency](/generative-engine-optimization-agency/) | Guarantees and vague reporting |
| None of these | Doing the work from the documentation | Skipping measurement |

The foundations are public. Google, OpenAI, Anthropic and Perplexity all document what they require, so a course mainly saves you time and gives you structure.

## What to do after the course

Apply what you learned to one site soon, while it is fresh.

Set a baseline first: record how your pages currently appear for the questions you care about, so you have something to compare against later. Then make one change at a time where you can. If you change six things at once, you will not know which one mattered.

Go back to the primary sources every so often. Platform guidance is updated, and a course recorded last year will not know what has changed since. When you need to reset your footing, the [principles section of our main guide](/#principles) sets out the approach this site recommends.
